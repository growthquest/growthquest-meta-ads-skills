# Ad Copy Frameworks

13 proven frameworks for ad scripts and copy. Each framework includes: when to use it, the structure with timing, which copywriter principles to layer in, and common mistakes.

The copywriter skill selects the right framework based on awareness stage, ad length, and format. Read the specific framework section when selected.

---

## Tier 1: The Big 5

### PAS (Problem-Agitate-Solve)

**When to use:** Problem Aware audiences. Short to medium length (15-60s scripts, ad primary text). Works across all formats.

**Structure:**
1. **Problem** (3-5s): State the pain specifically. "Neck pain that wakes you up at 3am" not "neck pain."
2. **Agitate** (5-10s): Turn up the cost of inaction. Make them feel it. What happens if they do nothing? What are they missing?
3. **Solve** (10-15s): Present your solution and mechanism. Why does this work? What's different about it?
4. **CTA** (2-3s): Clear, specific action.

**Principles to layer:**
- Hopkins specificity in the Problem section (exact numbers, exact scenarios)
- Schwartz desire channeling in the Solve section (lead with what they want, not what you built)

**Common mistakes:**
- Problem is too vague ("tired of wasting time?")
- Agitate section just restates the problem instead of escalating it
- Solution jumps to features instead of outcomes

---

### AIDA (Attention-Interest-Desire-Action)

**When to use:** Solution Aware to Product Aware audiences. Medium to long length (30-60s+ scripts). Strong for talking head and ad primary text.

**Structure:**
1. **Attention** (3-5s): Pattern interrupt hard. Something unexpected, counterintuitive, or emotionally charged.
2. **Interest** (5-10s): Create curiosity. Why should they care? What's the insight they don't know?
3. **Desire** (10-15s): Paint the feeling of having it. Lead with emotion, not features. Make them want the outcome.
4. **Action** (2-3s): Clear next step.

**Principles to layer:**
- Schwartz desire-first approach in the Desire section
- Cialdini social proof in the Interest section to build credibility
- Halbert pattern interrupt for the Attention opener

**Common mistakes:**
- Attention section is boring or predictable
- Interest and Desire blend together (Interest = curiosity, Desire = emotion)
- Skipping straight from Attention to features

---

### BAB (Before-After-Bridge)

**When to use:** Problem Aware audiences. Short to medium length (15-30s scripts, ad primary text, graphic copy). Best for visual transformation content.

**Structure:**
1. **Before** (3-5s): Show the struggle state. Make it relatable and specific.
2. **After** (5-8s): Show life with the solution. Paint the transformation.
3. **Bridge** (5-8s): Explain the mechanism. What changed? How did they get from Before to After?
4. **CTA** (2-3s): Clear action.

**Principles to layer:**
- Sugarman momentum so the Before-to-After transition feels inevitable
- Hopkins specificity in the Before (exact pain) and After (exact result)

**Common mistakes:**
- Before section is too generic
- After section overpromises without proof
- Bridge section is vague about the mechanism

---

### Hook-Story-Offer (Russell Brunson)

**When to use:** All awareness stages. Medium to long length (30-60s+). Most versatile framework. Strong for UGC, talking head, and ad primary text.

**Structure:**
1. **Hook** (1-3s): Pattern interrupt. "Wait." "This is weird but..." Something that stops the scroll.
2. **Story** (10-30s): Micro-story with problem, struggle, insight. Make it about THEM, not you. The story should mirror the audience's experience.
3. **Offer** (5-10s): Solution as the bridge from struggle to resolution. Position the product as the natural next step.
4. **CTA** (2-3s): Specific action.

**Principles to layer:**
- Halbert conversational tone throughout (short sentences, contractions, questions)
- Sugarman slippery slide (every sentence pulls to the next)
- Cialdini liking principle (relatable character in the story)

**Common mistakes:**
- Hook is too long or too polished (needs to feel raw)
- Story is about the brand instead of the audience
- Offer feels disconnected from the story's resolution

---

### PPPP (Promise-Picture-Proof-Push)

**When to use:** Product Aware to Most Aware audiences. Medium length (30-60s). Strong for ad primary text and retargeting. Only use when you have strong proof.

**Structure:**
1. **Promise** (5-8s): Biggest claim you can back up. Be specific. "Cut your CPA by 40% in 30 days."
2. **Picture** (10-15s): Paint the feeling of that promise being real. What does their day look like? How do they feel?
3. **Proof** (20-30s): Social proof, testimonials, numbers, case studies. This section is where skeptics convert. Stack multiple proof types.
4. **Push** (5-8s): Urgency or scarcity. Only genuine urgency, never fabricated.

**Principles to layer:**
- Hopkins specificity in the Promise (exact numbers, exact timeframes)
- Cialdini multiple proof types in the Proof section (testimonials + stats + authority)
- Cialdini scarcity in the Push (only if real)

**Common mistakes:**
- Promise is vague or unbelievable
- Picture section is skipped (jumping from promise to proof)
- Proof is thin (one testimonial instead of stacked evidence)
- Push uses fake urgency

## Tier 2: Advanced

### PASTOR (Ray Edwards)

**When to use:** Solution Aware to Product Aware audiences. Long format (60s+). Best for high-ticket offers with strong testimonials.

**Structure:**
1. **Problem** (5-8s): Identify and articulate their specific problem
2. **Agitate** (5-10s): Intensify the emotional weight
3. **Solution** (10-15s): Present the solution and mechanism
4. **Testimonial** (15-20s): Real customer proof. Must feel authentic. Include specific details from the customer's own voice, not polished marketing speak.
5. **Offer** (10-15s): What they get, what it costs, what's included
6. **Response** (5-8s): Specific CTA with risk reversal

**Principles to layer:**
- Cialdini social proof and authority in the Testimonial section
- Ogilvy brand image throughout (premium positioning for high-ticket)
- Hopkins specificity in the Offer section (exact deliverables, exact pricing)

**Common mistakes:**
- Testimonial section sounds scripted or polished
- No risk reversal in the Response section
- Treating this like PAS with a testimonial bolted on (the testimonial needs to be the centerpiece)

---

### ACCA (Awareness-Comprehension-Conviction-Action)

**When to use:** Skeptical audiences needing education. Medium to long format (30-60s+). Good for complex products or audiences who've been burned before.

**Structure:**
1. **Awareness** (5-10s): Make them aware of a problem or opportunity they haven't considered
2. **Comprehension** (10-15s): Help them understand WHY it matters and HOW it works
3. **Conviction** (15-20s): Build belief through evidence, logic, and authority
4. **Action** (5-8s): Clear, low-friction next step

**Principles to layer:**
- Hopkins specificity in the Comprehension section (concrete explanations)
- Cialdini authority in the Conviction section (credentials, expert endorsement)

**Common mistakes:**
- Comprehension section is too technical or too simple
- Conviction relies on assertions instead of evidence
- Trying to rush through Comprehension to get to the CTA

---

### SLAP (Stop-Look-Act-Purchase)

**When to use:** Most Aware audiences. Ultra-short format (6-15s). Designed for scroll-stoppers and quick-hit ads. Every second counts.

**Structure:**
1. **Stop** (1-2s): Extreme pattern interrupt. Visual or verbal shock.
2. **Look** (2-4s): Give them a reason to keep watching. One compelling fact or visual.
3. **Act** (2-4s): Drive immediate response. Urgency or value stack.
4. **Purchase** (2-3s): Direct CTA.

**Principles to layer:**
- Halbert pattern interrupt for the Stop
- Hopkins specificity (no room for vague language in 15 seconds)

**Common mistakes:**
- Trying to fit too much into 15 seconds
- Stop section doesn't actually stop the scroll
- Weak CTA (at this length, the CTA needs to be dead simple)

---

### FAB (Features-Advantages-Benefits)

**When to use:** Product Aware audiences. Short format (15-30s). Product-focused body copy and ad headlines. Good for feature launches or comparison ads.

**Structure:**
1. **Feature**: What it does. The technical fact. "12 amino acids per serving."
2. **Advantage**: Why that matters vs alternatives. "4x more coverage than competitors."
3. **Benefit**: What that means for their life. "Faster recovery, hit the gym more often."

**Principles to layer:**
- Hopkins specificity in Features (exact specs)
- Schwartz desire channeling in Benefits (connect to what they actually want)

**Common mistakes:**
- Stopping at Features (most common mistake)
- Advantages that aren't backed by data
- Benefits that are too generic ("save time")

---

### Star-Chain-Hook

**When to use:** Unaware audiences. Long format (60s+). Pure storytelling. Works for talking head and narrative video.

**Structure:**
1. **Star**: Introduce a relatable character. Use real names and specific details. The audience should see themselves.
2. **Chain**: Their struggle chain. A sequence of escalating problems or attempts that didn't work. Build tension.
3. **Hook**: The insight that changed everything. The moment they discovered the solution.

**Principles to layer:**
- Halbert conversational tone (this needs to feel like someone telling a story, not reading a script)
- Sugarman slippery slide (each part of the chain pulls to the next)
- Cialdini liking (the Star needs to be someone the audience relates to)

**Common mistakes:**
- Star is too perfect (needs to be relatable, with flaws)
- Chain doesn't escalate (each link should be worse than the last)
- Hook feels forced or unearned

## Tier 3: Performance

### UGC Direct Response

**When to use:** Problem Aware to Solution Aware audiences. Medium format (15-30s). Gold standard for TikTok and Reels. Fast, punchy, no breathing room.

**Structure:**
1. **Hook** (1-3s): Personal, immediate, scroll-stopping
2. **Benefit 1** (2-3s): First value point, stated as experience
3. **Benefit 2** (2-3s): Second value point
4. **Benefit 3** (2-3s): Third value point
5. **Brand Intro** (2-3s): Name drop and positioning
6. **CTA** (2-3s): Direct action

**Principles to layer:**
- Halbert conversational tone (must sound like a real person, not a brand)
- Hopkins specificity in each benefit (exact results, not vague claims)

**Common mistakes:**
- Sounds too polished or scripted
- Benefits are features in disguise
- Brand intro comes too early (loses the organic feel)
- Too much setup before the first benefit

---

### Hook-Body-CTA

**When to use:** All awareness stages. Short to medium format (15-30s). Simplest structure. Use when storytelling isn't needed and you just need to communicate value fast.

**Structure:**
1. **Hook** (2-4s): Grab attention with a strong opening statement, question, or stat
2. **Body** (10-30s): Problem + solution in a straightforward way. Can include 1-2 proof points.
3. **CTA** (2-3s): Clear next step

**Principles to layer:**
- Hopkins specificity throughout
- Sugarman momentum (even simple structures need flow)

**Common mistakes:**
- Hook is generic ("Are you tired of...?")
- Body tries to do too much
- No proof points in the body

---

### Harmon Brothers

**When to use:** Unaware to Problem Aware audiences. Long format (60-120s). Viral + conversion hybrid. Conversational, funny, self-aware.

**Structure:**
1. **Hook** (3-5s): Funny or shocking opening. Breaks convention.
2. **Problem** (10-15s): Relatable problem presented with humor. Make them laugh while they nod.
3. **Solution** (15-20s): Product introduction with personality. Demo the mechanism.
4. **Gain** (15-20s): Stack the benefits. Show the transformation. Use social proof.
5. **CTA** (5-8s): Clear action with value reminder.
6. **Scarcity** (3-5s): Genuine urgency or bonus.

**Principles to layer:**
- Halbert conversational tone (humor requires natural language)
- Cialdini liking (humor builds rapport)
- Hopkins specificity in the Gain section (even humor needs concrete claims)
- Ogilvy brand image (the humor should reinforce the brand personality, not undermine it)

**Common mistakes:**
- Humor overshadows the message
- Problem section isn't relatable enough
- Solution section is too serious after a funny opening (tonal whiplash)
- Trying to be funny without understanding the audience's humor style

## Quick Reference: Situation to Framework

| Situation | Best Frameworks |
|-----------|----------------|
| Cold audience, know nothing about us | Hook-Story-Offer, Star-Chain-Hook, Harmon Brothers |
| They know they have a problem | PAS, BAB, Hook-Story-Offer |
| They know solutions exist, comparing options | AIDA, PASTOR, PPPP, FAB |
| They know our product, need a push | PPPP, SLAP, Hook-Body-CTA |
| Ready to buy, need final nudge | SLAP, Hook-Body-CTA, Direct CTA |
| 6-15 second ad | SLAP, Hook-Body-CTA |
| 15-30 second ad | BAB, Hook-Body-CTA, UGC Direct Response |
| 30-60 second ad | PAS, AIDA, Hook-Story-Offer, PPPP |
| 60+ second ad | PASTOR, PPPP, Harmon Brothers, Star-Chain-Hook |
| UGC format | UGC Direct Response, Hook-Story-Offer, PAS |
| Talking head | PAS, AIDA, PASTOR, Hook-Story-Offer |
| Product demo | BAB, FAB, PPPP, Hook-Body-CTA |
| Retargeting | PPPP, Hook-Body-CTA, SLAP |
| VSL | PASTOR, Harmon Brothers, PPPP |
| Graphic / static image | PAS (compressed), BAB, SLAP, FAB |
| Ad primary text | PAS, AIDA, BAB, Hook-Story-Offer, PPPP |
| Headline | FAB (benefit-focused), Hook from any framework |

## Copywriter Principles Reference

These principles should be layered into whichever framework you select. Each framework section above notes which principles pair best, but you can apply any of them when the situation calls for it.

### Eugene Schwartz: Desire Channeling
Lead with desire. People don't want your product. They want the feeling it gives them. Open with the desire, not the mechanism. "Imagine closing a $50k deal" not "Our software tracks deal metrics." Identify which desire: money, status, health, time, belonging, power, peace.

### Joseph Sugarman: The Slippery Slide
Every sentence should make the next one feel inevitable. No padding, no filler. If you can remove a sentence and the script still works, remove it. Test by reading aloud. Does it flow like speech?

### Claude Hopkins: Specificity Rule
"23% increase" beats "significant improvement." "4,200 customers" beats "many customers." "47 seconds faster" beats "faster." "Surgical-grade stainless steel" beats "better quality." Numbers beat words. Always.

### Gary Halbert: Pattern Interrupt + Conversational Tone
Start sentences with 1-3 words: "Now, here's the thing." "So I asked myself." "Listen." Use contractions. Use questions: "Ever wondered why...?" Avoid corporate jargon. Write human.

### Robert Cialdini: 7 Persuasion Triggers
1. **Reciprocity**: Give value first (free trial, free audit, free tool)
2. **Commitment**: Small yes before big yes (quiz, assessment, micro-action)
3. **Social Proof**: Testimonials, numbers, authority ("10,000+ teams trust us")
4. **Authority**: Credentials, expert endorsement, certifications
5. **Liking**: Be personable, find common ground, mirror their language
6. **Scarcity**: Limited spots, deadline, exclusive access (only if genuine)
7. **Unity**: "For creators like us." Shared identity and belonging.

### David Ogilvy: Brand Image
The brand itself is the hero, not the features. Every tone and proof decision should reinforce the brand image you're building: premium, scrappy, scientific, community-driven. Specificity over cleverness. Long copy outperforms short copy when the copy is good.
